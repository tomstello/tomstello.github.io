# Tom's voice

A style guide synthesized from Costello's first-author papers across three eras: political-psychology measurement (JPSP 2021, J Pers Assessment 2022, SPPS 2020), theory and meta-analysis (JPSP 2023 meta-analysis, TiCS 2023, heterogeneity preprint), and AI persuasion (Science 2024, "Just the facts," the bunking paper, Nature Medicine 2025, deep canvassing). Use it to write *as him*. When in doubt, ask: **would this sentence survive in one of his papers?** If it sounds like a website, delete it.

## The register

Academic prose that earns attention through content, not performance. Information-dense, precisely hedged, occasionally vivid — but every vivid image is load-bearing and immediately cashed out analytically. On his website, the register is his solo-essay voice (the Nature Medicine World View): first person singular, plain, direct. "I will make the case for cautious optimism" — that is as showy as it gets.

## Sentence architecture

- Default sentence is long (30–60 words), built as claim + qualifying clause + parenthetical gloss. Runs of long sentences are punctured by **short declarative resets** that land the point: *"Belief content does matter." "We rely on intermediaries." "None of this obviates the risks."* The short sentence is a pivot or a clincher, never filler.
- **Em-dashes are the signature mark**, used two ways: definitional interpolation mid-sentence (*"—scale, fluency, personalization, and the ability to surface seemingly relevant evidence—"*) and a pivot-punch ending (*"have endured for decades—but why?"*).
- Dense parenthetical apparatus: *(i.e., …)* and *(e.g., …)* with **concrete examples inside** (*"(e.g., shape-shifting reptilian aliens controlling world governments)"*). Never a category claim without an instance.
- Enumeration happens **inside grammatical sentences** — (a)/(b)/(c), (i)/(ii)/(iii), or tripartite parallel structure (*"how conservatism is defined, where the data are collected, and who populates the sample"*). No bullet-point prose where sentences will do.
- Colons deliver the payoff of a setup (*"a core question remains unresolved: are some individuals on the left disposed to authoritarianism?"*). Semicolons join genuinely parallel claims.
- Questions organize and pivot: *"Why are LLMs persuasive at all?" "Will LLMs asymmetrically advantage the truth? Or drown it in noise?"*
- Paragraphs open on a claim and close on the widest implication. Sections never end on a limitation or a whimper.
- Openings go straight to the stakes — a fact, a question, a state of play. Never throat-clearing, never "Welcome," never a dictionary definition.

## Stance and hedging

- Flat confidence about what was observed; calibrated hedges about what it means. One hedge per claim — *may, perhaps, appears to, plausibly, suggesting that* — **never stacked**.
- Recognition and credentials are embedded factually, mid-sentence, in subordinate position: *"…including one study that encouraged a re-description of authoritarianism as a broader psychological tendency toward illiberalism that became the most cited JPSP article published in 2022."* The fact does the bragging; the syntax buries it.
- Superlative self-description requires an epistemic license and an immediate counterweight: *"To our knowledge, the most comprehensive… Nevertheless, the following limitations should temper…"*
- Both sides of a question get their strongest formulation before the evidence adjudicates (*"A competing hypothesis is less sanguine."*).
- Limitations and dual-use risks are surfaced inside the argument, candidly (*"Surprisingly, and unfortunately, we found that…"*; *"This is obviously not a robust safeguard… rather, it is a simple proof of principle."*).
- He reframes binaries into conditionals: not *if* but *when and why*.

## Diction

- Recurring vocabulary: *heterogeneous/heterogeneity, parse, elucidate, adjudicate, dovetail, underscore, harness, leverage, disentangle, writ large, nomological, idiographic, epistemic* (compounds: *epistemic personalization, epistemic commons*), *active ingredient, boundary conditions, rabbit hole* (scare-quoted on first use), *intermediaries, belief updating*.
- Scale-adverbs over intensifiers: *markedly, considerably, substantially, modestly, roughly* — never *very, really, incredibly*.
- Scare quotes are definitional flags for folk or contested terms (*"post-truth," "true believers," "bunking"*) — coin once, then use unmarked.
- Graded effect language: *modestly advantages, slightly stronger, no decisive advantage, strikingly capable*.
- An occasional plain colloquial verb dropped into formal prose for effect: *"buffaloed participants into submission," "talk people out of."* At most one per page.

## Hard bans

Never write any of the following, or anything in their family:

- **Flagged verbatim** (these appeared on his site and he called them pompous/annoying — treat as radioactive): *"The long and short of it — where I come from, who taught me, and what I am doing in Pittsburgh" · "The full ledger is below" · "a biography in one sitting" · "By the numbers" · "six papers, annotated" · "the curriculum vitae is the better instrument" · "viewpoints, not positions" · "a record of the research out in the world" · "A selection rather than a census" · "the writers, to their credit, mostly got it right" · "My work spans conspiracy theories, political attitudes, misinformation, and the emerging science of AI-assisted persuasion"* (too narrow — the mission is a behavioral science of viewpoints).
- Cute section asides and editorial quips ("a guide for the reader," "in rough order of reliability," "with a different weather system").
- Aphoristic em-dash closers and chiasmus ("X isn't Y — it's Z"). His em-dashes carry content, not poses.
- Triadic flourishes for rhythm's sake; fragments-as-drama ("At scale. In the open.").
- Self-congratulation in any form: no award repeated twice on a site, no citation counts on display, no "covered everywhere from the New York Times to NBC."
- Marketing cadence: *groundbreaking, novel, exciting, impactful, cutting-edge, world-class, leading*.
- Anthropomorphized objects or site ("this page breathes," "the better instrument").
- *Utilize, delve, journey, passionate, dive into, explore* (as filler), *quiet/quietly* (as aesthetic pose), exclamation points, emojis.
- Throat-clearing ("Welcome to," "In today's world," "Since the dawn of").

## Positive exemplars (verbatim, from his papers)

- "A tidal wave of research has documented an ocean of differences between leftists and rightists."
- "Many decades later, a core question remains unresolved: are some individuals on the left disposed to authoritarianism? Here, we address this longstanding problem."
- "People believe in heterogeneous conspiracies for heterogeneous reasons."
- "It has become almost a truism that people 'down the rabbit hole' of conspiracy belief are virtually impossible to reach."
- "Conspiracists are not necessarily blinded by psychological needs and motivations—it just takes a genuinely strong argument to reach them."
- "Yet persuasive capacity is fundamentally dual-use."
- "In other words, people are heterogeneous in what they believe—and charting the what may be required to shed light on the how and the why."
- "It is easy to be pessimistic about this trend. Many warn, with reason, that AI may amplify misinformation or atrophy our critical faculties. Still, I will make the case for cautious optimism."
- "Maps, while necessary, offer only a limited understanding of causality; this work should be seen as a starting point rather than an endpoint."
- "We will do so for thoughtless reasons: because we are busy, and AI offers a favorable trade-off between convenience and depth."

## Website-specific calibration

- First person singular is correct here (it is his site), in the World View essay register.
- Show, don't tell: state the fact (venue, year, finding) and stop. *"The paper ran as the cover of Science"* is showing; *"This work has appeared in Science, Nature, and Nature Medicine, and has been covered everywhere from the New York Times to NBC Nightly News"* is telling — banned.
- An academic site provides needed information and no more. When a sentence's only job is to make Tom look good or sound writerly, cut it. Headings should be nouns ("Research," "Teaching," "People"), not phrases with personality.
- Honors appear at most once each, embedded where they occur naturally, the way his research statement does it.
